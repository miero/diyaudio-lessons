A 1 ms impulse response has no hard lowest frequency

A short record has coarse unpadded DFT bins and may cut off a slow resonance, but neither fact creates a frequency below which its transform ceases to exist. Use the same 1 ms / 48 kHz record first as a single-tap impulse, then as a truncated damped resonance, to separate what duration T sets from what sample rate Fs sets. Two further views map the chosen decay criterion across frequency/Q and show how the tail and spectral error converge as T grows.

The precise claim

  1. Finite duration is not a low-frequency cutoff. For the single-tap impulse h[n] = δ[n], the discrete-time Fourier transform is H(ejω) = 1 at every frequency, including DC.
  2. Duration T sets spacing and a characteristic detail scale. An unpadded N-point DFT samples the continuous DTFT every Fs/N = 1/T Hz. If a nonzero tail is cut at T, the rectangular truncation kernel also has characteristic width about 1/T. Zero-padding fills in the DTFT between bins; it does not restore an omitted tail.
  3. Sample rate Fs sets Nyquist and sample count. At fixed T, N = FsT and Nyquist is Fs/2. Raising Fs gives more samples and higher bandwidth, but does not change 1/T or contain a decay sooner.
Correction to a common shortcut: “a T-second IR cannot encode below 1/T” confuses DFT sample spacing or truncation blur with a hard frequency boundary. A finite sequence has a continuous, periodic DTFT. The meaningful question for a resonance is whether its omitted tail is small enough for a stated criterion.

Bounds and takeaways

The damped-resonance criterion

For the model used in the demo, with amplitude envelope measured relative to its value at n = 0,

h[n] = e−n/(Fsτ) cos(2πf0n/Fs)  ;  τ = Q/(πf0)
ε = 10(target dB)/20  ;  T ≥ τ ln(1/ε)  ⇔  Q ≤ πf0T / ln(1/ε)

For fixed Q the same condition can be written f0 ≥ Q ln(1/ε)/(πT). This is a containment bound for a chosen amplitude target, not a universal minimum frequency or universal maximum/minimum Q. Change −20 dB to −60 dB and the bound changes. A noise floor, acceptable spectral error, or energy criterion would produce a different bound.

Explicit parameter bounds — representation is not estimation

ParameterRepresentation/model domainCriterion or estimation rule
f0 For a real sampled cosine, 0 ≤ f0 ≤ Fs/2; the upper bound is Nyquist. Duration T does not add a positive lower bound. An identification method may choose k samples per cycle, giving f0 ≤ Fs/k. The choice of k is a practical robustness rule, not a digital representation law.
τ τ > 0, with no positive storage lower bound: as τ becomes very short, later samples simply approach zero and the sequence approaches one nonzero tap. Containment to amplitude ε requires τ ≤ T/ln(1/ε). If an estimator demands m samples per time constant, τ ≥ m/Fs is a user-chosen estimation robustness rule, not a representation law.
Q For f0 > 0, Q can approach zero from above in this damped-cosine parameterization; there is no universal positive lower bound. Containment gives Q ≤ πf0T/ln(1/ε). This upper bound changes with f0, T, and the chosen ε.

This lesson uses Q = πf0τ as its ringiness convention. It is the standard physical second-order relation in the lightly damped approximation; at very low Q, the exact natural frequency, damped frequency, damping ratio, and physical second-order use of “Q” need more care. The envelope and containment equations above remain the definitions of this lesson’s damped-cosine model. Rules such as a fixed number of samples per cycle or time constant are therefore declared estimation choices—not hard limits such as an arbitrary Fs/4 cutoff.

What duration T does tell you

  • The exact unpadded DFT sample spacing, about 1/T.
  • The characteristic spectral-detail and rectangular-truncation scale, about 1/T.
  • How far a physical decay has been observed, and therefore whether a stated tail criterion is met.

What it does not tell you

  • It does not create a brick-wall lower frequency limit.
  • It does not say a short record is wrong when the true IR is already zero; the one-tap impulse is the clean counterexample.
  • It does not define a universal Q bound without f0 and a decay/error criterion.

The resonance curves are an exact discrete-time model. Real measurements add noise, reflections, windows, multiple modes, and uncertainty about when “small enough” has been reached. Test convergence with longer records whenever possible.
